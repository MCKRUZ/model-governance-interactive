Soft spot — worth raising
The competitor clause
If a rival releases something risky without similar protections, the provider may relax its own rules. It has to confirm the situation really changed, say publicly that it adjusted, and check it does not make things meaningfully worse.
Left out of the framework
Persuasion
Handled somewhere else on purpose: in the rules about how the model should behave, in limits on political campaigning and lobbying, and in investigations into influence campaigns.
Cadence
Automatic often, experts now and then
Models change bit by bit now, not just in big training runs, so automatic tests run constantly. Experts step in periodically to check the automatic tests are still measuring the right things.
04 — The testing

Two very different questions

The product asks whether a normal person will get something harmful out of it. The testing team asks what the model can do if someone really tries, with tools and time. A model can look well behaved in the app and still be near the top of the risk scale underneath.

A few exceptions: some hacking challenges come from public security research, and independent outside testers publish some of their own methods.
Is the answer checker a fixed rule?
No. It is a small trained model, so it still gets things wrong in both directions. It is steadier than the main model only because it has one narrow question to answer instead of writing anything at all.
The firm part comes next. Once it produces a score, blocking or allowing is a plain cutoff. A judgment call underneath, a hard rule on top. That is the shape a company needs in front of anything an AI agent does that matters.
When customers train the model themselves
Training the model further on your own data edits the same part of it that holds the refusals. Research has shown that even ordinary, harmless data can wear those refusals down by accident. So the safety report written for the original model may no longer describe the model you end up with.

05 — The gap

Where model safety stops

All of the provider's work answers one question: is this model, on its own, safe to release. It says nothing about what happens once the model is wired into a company's systems, data and approvals. Two different problems. Only one has a good answer today.

The supplier — whoever builds the model

Model governance, which is what the provider does, and enterprise AI governance, which is what a company deploying the model needs, are related but genuinely different disciplines.
Many companies are quietly assuming the provider's safety work stands in for their own governance program. It does not, and the three gaps above show why.
Why the timing matters
As providers push toward assistants that work on their own for longer, the risk changes shape. A wrong answer is embarrassing. An assistant with real access making its own multi-step mistake is not. That risk sits with the company using it.
Building that layer inside a company is a services problem, not a model problem. It needs to know that company's systems, its appetite for risk, its legal duties and how it manages change.
Our agentic delivery methodology already does this: staged approvals, limited permissions, checks along the way, and a way to undo. Existing work, made more necessary by where the providers are heading.

The one idea underneath all of it
Nobody writes an AI model's abilities the way a programmer writes an app. There is no line of code that says "know how to do X."
The model reads an enormous amount of text and learns to predict the next word. Then people give it feedback to shape it: be helpful, follow instructions, refuse harmful requests.
As models get bigger they spontaneously get better at things nobody trained them to do. Some of those things are risky, and nobody intended them.
Because nobody wrote the risky ability on purpose, you cannot read the code to find it. You have to test the model and push it until you find its ceiling. That single fact is why everything else in here exists.
